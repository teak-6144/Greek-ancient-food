The Ancient Greek Table
Food in Classical Greece, about 500 to 300 BCE

What the ancient Greeks ate

Mostly barley, olives, wine, figs and cheese. Fish came from the sea when you lived near it, and meat was something you ate at a festival.

The short answer

Three crops carried the whole diet

Greek hillsides are thin and dry. Barley, olive trees and grapevines grow there anyway, and almost every meal was built from them.

σῖτος
sitos, grain

Barley first, wheat for those who could pay. Eaten as dough, flatbread, loaves and porridge.

ἔλαιον
elaion, olive oil

The main cooking fat, a dressing for greens and bread, and also lamp fuel and body lotion.

οἶνος
oinos, wine

Drunk daily by adults and almost always mixed with water. Neat wine was considered barbarian.

Greeks sorted a meal into two parts. Sitos was the grain base. Opson (ὄψον) was anything eaten with it: cheese, olives, vegetables, fish, and later any kind of meat. Fish was so loved that the word opsophagos came to mean a fish-lover.

A day at the table

Three meals, one big one

Writers disagree on the names across the centuries, but the Classical Athenian pattern ran like this.

Morning

ἀκράτισμα akratisma

Barley bread dipped in undiluted wine. Figs or olives on the side if there were any.

Midday

ἄριστον ariston

A quick, light meal. Bread or maza with cheese, olives or whatever was left from the night before.

Evening

δεῖπνον deipnon

The main meal. Grain with a relish such as lentil soup, boiled greens, salt fish or cheese, with watered wine.

At a banquet the symposium followed dinner. The tables were cleared and guests drank mixed wine from a shared bowl while nibbling tragemata: roasted chickpeas, nuts, figs, cheese and honey cakes.

Who ate what, and where

Wealth and place changed the plate

Meat came with ritual

Animals were sacrificed to the gods at festivals, and the roasted meat was shared out among the crowd. For many people that was the only beef or pork they ate all month. Richer households added game birds, hare and imported luxuries such as dates.

Sparta ate differently

Spartan men took their meals together in common messes. A famous dish was the black broth (melas zomos), a pork stew with vinegar and salt that outsiders mocked as tasteless.

The coast ate fish

Port cities such as Athens had busy fish markets selling sardines, anchovies and tuna, much of it salted so it could travel inland. Farming villages in the hills leaned on cheese, beans and wild greens instead.

Not yet on the table

Tomatoes, potatoes, corn, peppers and chocolate came from the Americas after 1492. Cane sugar, coffee, lemons and oranges were also absent from the Classical diet.

Try it

A Greek dinner you can make tonight

Everything here was available in Classical Athens. Serves four.

Ingredients

  • 250 g brown or green lentils
  • 1 onion and 3 cloves of garlic
  • 3 tablespoons olive oil, plus extra to serve
  • 1 tablespoon vinegar and a pinch of dried oregano
  • Barley flatbread or a crusty loaf
  • Sheep or goat cheese, olives, fresh or dried figs
  • A little honey

Method

  1. Simmer the lentils with the chopped onion and garlic in plenty of water for about 30 minutes, until soft and thick.
  2. Stir in the olive oil, vinegar, oregano and salt.
  3. Serve in bowls with bread for dipping.
  4. Put cheese, olives and figs on the table as the opson, with a spoonful of honey over the figs.

How we know. The picture comes from Athenaeus's Deipnosophistae (about 200 CE), the food poem of Archestratus of Gela (4th century BCE), Homer, Aristophanes' comedies, and archaeobotany, the study of ancient seeds and bones.

Most written evidence describes Athens and men with money. Village and household diets are mainly known from archaeology, so frequencies on this page are general estimates.
